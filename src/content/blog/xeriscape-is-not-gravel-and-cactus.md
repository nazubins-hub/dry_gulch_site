The word "xeriscape" still carries a reputation problem. Most people picture a front yard of white rock and a few spiny plants baking in the sun. That's not what water-wise design looks like in practice — and it's especially not what it looks like at 7,000+ feet, where the plant palette is completely different from what desert xeriscaping uses.

## Seven principles, not one look

Xeriscape is a design method, not an aesthetic: thoughtful planning, soil improvement, efficient irrigation, practical turf areas, appropriate plant selection, mulch, and ongoing maintenance. None of those principles dictate gravel. They dictate matching water use to what a plant actually needs, so irrigation isn't fighting the site.

## What it looks like in the valley

A well-designed xeric planting bed here can be lush-looking for most of the season — native grasses, penstemon, yarrow, rabbitbrush, and low sedums layered the way a naturalistic perennial border would be, just built around species that don't need daily irrigation once established. Turf isn't eliminated, it's placed only where it earns its water budget: a small usable lawn area instead of wall-to-wall Kentucky bluegrass across a half-acre lot.

## The real payoff

The visible result is a yard that still reads as green and intentional. The invisible result is a irrigation system running a fraction of the hours, healthier soil that holds moisture longer between waterings, and a landscape that isn't fighting the valley's climate every August.
